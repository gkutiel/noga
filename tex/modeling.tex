\subsection{Problem Formulation}

In day-ahead electricity procurement, a market participant must commit to a
quantity $p_t$ for every settlement slot $t$ of the following day before
observing the actual demand $y_t$.  Committed energy is paid at the day-ahead
price; any mismatch is settled after the fact at less favourable prices.  We
model $y_t$ as a random variable whose conditional distribution is
\emph{unknown} and must be learned from data; we make no parametric assumption
about its form.

\paragraph*{Price-based cost.}  Let $\pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t$ denote the day-ahead
price, $\pi^{u}_t$ the price paid for each unit of shortfall (under-prediction),
and $\pi^{o}_t$ the cost of each unit of surplus (over-prediction), with
\begin{equation}
    \pi^{u}_t \;>\; \pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t \;>\; \pi^{o}_t \;>\; 0.
    \label{eq:price-order}
\end{equation}
The total realised cost of procuring $p_t$ when demand turns out to be $y_t$ is
\begin{equation}
    C_t(p_t, y_t) =
        \pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t \min(y_t, p_t)
        + \pi^{u}_t \,(y_t - p_t)^{+}
        + \pi^{o}_t \,(p_t - y_t)^{+},
    \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
where $(z)^{+}=\max(0,z)$.  Since the consumed energy $\pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t y_t$
must be paid regardless of the forecast, it is convenient to work with the
\emph{excess cost} above a perfect forecast,
\begin{equation}
    E_t(p_t, y_t) = C_t(p_t,y_t) - \pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t y_t
    = \underbrace{(\pi^{u}_t - \pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t)}_{c^{u}_t}\,(y_t - p_t)^{+}
    + \underbrace{\pi^{o}_t}_{c^{o}_t}\,(p_t - y_t)^{+}.
    \label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:excess} is a pinball loss whose two slopes, $c^u_t$ and
$c^o_t$, are \emph{prices} that vary from slot to slot.

\paragraph*{Information structure.}  The day-ahead price is the
market-clearing price (the Israeli system marginal price, SMP).  It is set
\emph{after} suppliers decide how much electricity to buy, so none of the
prices $\pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t$, $\pi^u_t$, $\pi^o_t$ is known when $p_t$ is
chosen.  The forecaster observes only the information $\mathcal{I}_t$ that our
models use as inputs: recent demand, weather and the calendar.  Prices are
observed after the fact, so historical prices \emph{are} available for
training.

\paragraph*{Goal.}  The objective is to choose $p_t$ that minimises the
\emph{expected total cost} $\mathbb{E}[C_t \mid \mathcal{I}_t]$ given the
information available at decision time---not the expected prediction error.

\subsection{Optimal Decision: a Cost-Weighted Quantile}%
\label{sec:newsvendor}

For fixed, known prices, Equation~\eqref{eq:excess} is the classical
\emph{newsvendor} problem~\parencite{arrow1951optimal}, whose solution is the
critical fractile
\begin{equation}
    p^*_t = F_t^{-1}(\tau_t), \qquad
    \tau_t = \frac{c^{u}_t}{c^{u}_t + c^{o}_t}
           = \frac{\pi^{u}_t - \pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t}
                  {\pi^{u}_t - \pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t + \pi^{o}_t},
    \label{eq:optimal_threshold}
\end{equation}
where $F_t$ is the conditional distribution of demand.  When the prices are
themselves unknown, setting the derivative of
$\mathbb{E}[E_t(p,y_t)\mid\mathcal{I}_t]$ to zero gives the condition
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\big[c^u_t\,\mathbb{1}\{y_t > p^*_t\} \,\big|\, \mathcal{I}_t\big]
    = \mathbb{E}\big[c^o_t\,\mathbb{1}\{y_t < p^*_t\} \,\big|\, \mathcal{I}_t\big]:
    \label{eq:weighted_quantile}
\end{equation}
the optimal forecast is a \emph{cost-weighted} quantile of demand, in which
each possible outcome is weighted by the price it would be settled at.  Three
consequences motivate this paper:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The target quantile is not constant.}  The cost asymmetry
          $c^u_t/c^o_t$ varies with the market price, so the cost-optimal
          quantile varies across slots.  A model trained with a symmetric loss
          (targeting the mean or median) or with a fixed-ratio pinball loss
          (targeting a constant quantile) cannot track it.
    \item \textbf{Prices and demand are correlated.}  High demand is what
          drives high prices, so the scenarios in which the forecast falls
          short are also those in which shortfall is most expensive.
          Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted_quantile} accounts for this joint
          behaviour; a forecast that is first trained without prices and then
          shifted cannot.
    \item \textbf{Errors in expensive slots matter more.}  The slopes of
          Equation~\eqref{eq:excess} scale with the price level, so a
          megawatt-hour of error during a price spike costs many times more
          than one at night.  A cost-trained model allocates its capacity
          accordingly; an MSE-trained model weighs all slots equally.
\end{enumerate}
The prices cannot be model inputs, but they can be used in the training loss.
A \emph{unified} model trained on Equation~\eqref{eq:excess} with the
realised historical prices learns, from the observable features, when prices
and hence the cost asymmetry tend to be high, and targets the cost-weighted
quantile of Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted_quantile} directly.

\subsection{Synthetic Imbalance Prices}
\label{sec:synthetic-prices}

The day-ahead prices in our study are the \emph{real} Israeli SMP values
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset}).  Real imbalance prices, however, are not
published for the Israeli market, so we generate synthetic over- and
under-prediction prices that satisfy Equation~\eqref{eq:price-order} and the
following stylised facts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the cost of surplus is roughly a fixed fraction of the day-ahead
          price;
    \item the price of shortfall is a non-linear, convex and noisy function of
          the day-ahead price---scarcity is expensive exactly when the system
          is already tight;
    \item the under/over price ratio $\pi^u_t/\pi^o_t$ lies in $[5, 20]$ and is
          roughly increasing in the day-ahead price, in line with the cost
          asymmetries reported for electricity
          procurement~\parencite{zhang2022costoriented, weron2014electricity}.
\end{itemize}
Concretely, for each 30-minute settlement period we draw
\begin{align}
    \pi^{o}_t &= \alpha\,(1+\varepsilon_t)\,\pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t,
        & \varepsilon_t &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_o^2), \\
    r_t &= \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(
        \big(r_{\min} + (r_{\max}-r_{\min})\,u_t^{\gamma}\big)\,e^{\eta_t},\;
        r_{\min},\, r_{\max}\Big),
        & \eta_t &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2), \\
    \pi^{u}_t &= r_t\,\pi^{o}_t,
\end{align}
where $u_t\in[0,1]$ is the position of $\log\pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t$ between its
5th and 99th percentiles.  We use $\alpha=0.3$, $\gamma=2$, $\sigma_o=0.03$,
$\sigma_u=0.15$, $r_{\min}=5$ and $r_{\max}=20$.  Because $\alpha r_{\min}>1$,
the ordering~\eqref{eq:price-order} holds in every slot, and $\pi^u_t =
\alpha(1+\varepsilon_t)\,r_t\,\pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t$ is the day-ahead price times
an increasing convex multiplier.  None of these prices is a model input; they
enter only the training loss and the evaluation.  Figure~\ref{fig:tau-vs-price} shows the resulting
cost-optimal quantile: it ranges from about $0.6$ in cheap night-time slots to
about $0.94$ during price spikes.  Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} varies all
generator parameters.

\paragraph*{Scarcity variant.}  In the base generator the imbalance price
depends on the day-ahead price only.  In real balancing markets, however, the
price of shortfall is highest when the \emph{whole system} is short.  For a
national-scale forecaster this matters: its own under-prediction tends to
coincide with a system-wide shortfall.  As a robustness check we therefore
correlate the noise $\eta_t$ with the system-wide demand surprise
$s_t = (y_t - \hat y^{\mathrm{Noga}}_t)/\hat y^{\mathrm{Noga}}_t$, averaged over
the settlement period, while keeping its variance:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_t = \sigma_u\left(\rho\, z_t + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\xi_t\right),
    \qquad \xi_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),
\end{equation}
where $z_t$ is the robustly standardised surprise (median and median
absolute deviation, clipped at $\pm4$).  Since $s_t$ depends on the realised
demand, the scarcity premium couples the price of shortfall with the
forecast error itself.  For $\rho=0.6$, the under/over ratio in periods when
the system is short is 8--15\% higher than in periods with the same day-ahead
price when it is long.  We report $\rho\in\{0.3,0.6,0.9\}$.
