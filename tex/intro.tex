Electricity for the next day is bought before the demand is known.  Energy
committed in the day-ahead market is paid at the day-ahead price; any
shortfall must be bought in real time at a higher price, and any surplus is
sold back at a lower one~\parencite{stoft2002power, kirschen2018fundamentals}.
A megawatt-hour short typically costs five to twenty times more than a
megawatt-hour too much~\parencite{zhang2022costoriented}, and the ratio is
itself driven by the price: shortfalls are most expensive exactly when the
system is tight and the day-ahead price is high.  The cost-optimal forecast is
therefore not the expected demand but a quantile of it, and the right quantile
\emph{moves with the price} from one settlement period to the next
(Section~\ref{sec:modeling}).

Forecasting practice does not reflect this.  Demand forecasts, including the
operational forecast of the Israeli system operator studied here, are built
for accuracy and are close to unbiased, so they fall short about half of the
time.  Price forecasts are also built for accuracy, separately.  The
cost-oriented load-forecasting literature trains demand models on an
asymmetric loss, but with a \emph{fixed} cost ratio that ignores prices; and
the models that forecast load and price jointly are again trained only for
accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:related}).  No existing model lets the demand
forecast follow the price.

We close this gap with a \emph{unified} price-and-demand model.  One small
neural network reads only information available a day ahead---calendar,
weather, yesterday's demand and yesterday's price---and computes a shared
representation from which two heads forecast the demand and the three prices
of the slot (day-ahead, shortfall and surplus).  The demand forecast is trained
on the realised procurement cost, priced at the realised prices, while the
price head is trained to predict those prices.  Because both heads read the
same features, the demand head learns its safety margin from features that
encode when prices---and hence shortfalls---will be expensive.

\paragraph*{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The first unified price-and-demand model.}  To the best of
          our knowledge, this is the first model that forecasts electricity
          demand and prices together \emph{and} trains the demand forecast on
          the price-weighted procurement cost.  On the Israeli test year it
          has the lowest cost of all models we tested, \$49.5 million: 6\%
          below the strongest alternative, a joint load-and-price network with
          the same architecture trained for accuracy and corrected to the cost
          afterwards, and 19\% below the operator's forecast after the best
          correction ($p\le0.008$ in every comparison).  Its safety margin
          rises with the price, as theory demands; the margin of a
          fixed-asymmetry model falls.

    \item \textbf{Real money.}  Replacing the operator's published forecast
          would save about \$27 million per year in Israel---\$2.65 per
          resident and 0.86\% of the day-ahead energy bill---mostly by
          avoiding shortfalls.  Projected to a market the size of the United
          States, the same improvement is worth roughly \$1 billion per year.

    \item \textbf{Small and cheap.}  The model has fewer than 2{,}000
          parameters, trains in about five minutes on a standard laptop
          without a GPU, and needs only 21 months of training data.

    \item \textbf{An open dataset and benchmark.}  We release a new dataset of
          the Israeli market that aligns 5-minute national demand, the
          operator's \emph{real} day-ahead forecast, real half-hourly
          day-ahead prices and weather (April~2023--December~2025), a
          generator of price-dependent imbalance prices, and the complete
          code.\footnote{\codeurl}
\end{enumerate}
