Figure~\ref{fig:model} shows the model.  It forecasts the demand of a
5-minute slot $t$ of the next day, together with the slot's three prices, from
information available a day in advance.

\paragraph*{Inputs.}  (i)~The \emph{demand history}: the ten 5-minute
readings ending exactly 24~hours before $t$.  (ii)~The \emph{calendar}: month,
day of week and 5-minute slot of the day, each a learned two-dimensional
embedding with norm at most~1.  (iii)~The \emph{weather}: temperature,
wet-bulb temperature and relative humidity at Haifa, Jerusalem and Tel~Aviv,
for slot $t$ and for the same slot of the previous day.  (iv)~\emph{Yesterday's
day-ahead price} of the same slot, $\pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_{t-24\text{h}}$, which is
public when the forecast is made.  Weather and price are standardised with
training-set statistics.

\paragraph*{Shared representation.}  One projection,
$\mathrm{Linear}(9\to4)$ with a LeakyReLU, maps today's and yesterday's weather
with the same weights, so the network can compare the same learned weather
features on both days---which explains the change in demand since yesterday.
The embeddings, both projected weather vectors and yesterday's price (15
values) pass through three layers of width 16, each followed by a LeakyReLU,
layer normalisation and dropout (0.1).  The result is a vector of shared
features $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$, read by two heads.

\paragraph*{Demand head.}  A linear layer maps $\mathbf{z}$ to ten attention
logits $\mathbf{o}$, a log-scale $s$ and a shift $\tilde b$:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_t \;=\; a\sum_{k=1}^{10} \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{o})_k\,h_k
              \;+\; b, \qquad a=e^{s},\quad b=\tilde b\,\sigma_y,
    \label{eq:forecast}
\end{equation}
where $h_k$ are the history readings and $\sigma_y$ is the training-set
standard deviation of the day-over-day demand change.  The attention picks the
starting point from yesterday's demand; $a$ and $b$ set the level, including
the safety margin.

\paragraph*{Price head.}  A second linear layer maps the \emph{same}
features to the three prices,
$(\hat\pi^{\mathrm{DA}}_t,\hat\pi^{o}_t,\hat\pi^{u}_t) =
\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}} \odot \exp(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{c})$, with
$\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}$ the training-set mean prices.  It has no other input:
to predict the prices, $\mathbf{z}$ itself must encode when prices---and hence
shortfalls---will be expensive, and the demand head reads exactly these
features.  This is what makes the model \emph{unified}.

\paragraph*{Training.}  The loss is the mean excess cost of the demand
forecast (Equation~\eqref{eq:excess}) at the realised prices plus the mean
relative squared error of the three price forecasts,
$\tfrac13\sum_j((\hat\pi^j_t-\pi^j_t)/\pi^j_t)^2$, with equal weight.  We use
Adam~\parencite{kingma2014adam} with learning rate $2\times10^{-3}$, batches of
5{,}000 slots and a fixed budget of 500 epochs (the held-out cost is
dominated by a few price spikes and is too noisy for early stopping).

\paragraph*{Small and efficient.}  The model has \nParams{} parameters.  On a
standard laptop (Intel Core i5-1135G7, no GPU) a full training run over the
\nTrain{} training slots takes about five minutes, and forecasting a year
takes a fraction of a second, so the model can be retrained daily without
special hardware.
