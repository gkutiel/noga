\begin{abstract}
Day-ahead electricity is bought before demand is known: committed energy is
paid at the day-ahead price, and any shortfall or surplus is settled later at
imbalance prices.  A shortfall costs several times more than a surplus, and
\emph{how much} more depends on the price of the hour, so the cost-optimal
forecast is a quantile of demand that moves with the price.  Forecasting
practice nevertheless keeps demand and price apart: demand models and price
models are each trained for accuracy, and cost-oriented demand models use a
fixed cost ratio that ignores prices.  We propose, to our knowledge, the first
\emph{unified} model: one small neural network that forecasts the demand and
the prices of every slot from a shared representation and trains the demand
forecast on the price-weighted procurement cost, so that its safety margin
follows its own expectation of when shortfalls will be expensive.  On a new
open dataset of the Israeli market---5-minute national demand, the system
operator's real day-ahead forecast, real day-ahead prices and weather---with
imbalance prices generated from the real day-ahead price, the unified model
lowers the excess procurement cost of the 2025 test year to \$49.5 million,
significantly below demand-only models trained for accuracy or with a fixed
cost asymmetry, 6\% below a joint demand-and-price network trained for
accuracy and corrected to the cost afterwards, and 19\% below the operator's
forecast after the best correction.  Replacing the operator's published
forecast would save about \$27 million per year in Israel (\$2.65 per
resident, 0.86\% of the day-ahead energy bill), which projects to roughly
\$1 billion per year for a market the size of the United States.  The model has
fewer than 2{,}000 parameters and trains in five minutes on a laptop.
\end{abstract}
