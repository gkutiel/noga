\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All models share the same architecture and training procedure described in
Section~\ref{sec:method}; they differ only in their training objective.
We compare four training strategies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{L2 (MSE)} — trained with mean squared error.
    \item \textbf{L1 (MAE)} — trained with mean absolute error.
    \item \textbf{Pinball} — trained with the standard (linear) pinball loss.
    \item \textbf{PLF} — trained end-to-end with the piecewise linear asymmetric
          cost function (Section~\ref{sec:modeling}).
\end{itemize}

To isolate the benefit of end-to-end cost-aware training, we additionally
evaluate \emph{post-hoc calibrated} variants of all four models
(Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}), in which an affine transformation
$g(z)=\alpha z + \beta$ is fitted on the training set to minimise the
expected asymmetric cost.

Models are evaluated on four cost functions that reflect different assumptions
about the cost of over- and under-prediction: the piecewise linear cost
(\textbf{PLF}), the L1 cost, the L2 cost, and the standard pinball loss.
The primary metric is the \textbf{PLF} cost, which best reflects the
asymmetric, non-linear structure of real-world electricity procurement costs.

The PLF and pinball functions penalise under-predictions more heavily than

\subsection{Discussion}

\paragraph{Cost-aware training outperforms symmetric baselines.}
Without calibration, the PLF model achieves the lowest PLF cost ($0.386$),
compared to $0.407$ for L1 and $0.428$ for L2 — reductions of $5\%$ and
$10\%$ respectively.
The pinball model also underperforms on the PLF metric ($0.438$), suggesting
that a linear asymmetric loss is insufficient to capture the non-linear cost
structure.
The improvement of the PLF model comes entirely from aligning the training
objective with the operational cost: the model learns to shift its predictions
upward in a feature-adaptive way, precisely where under-prediction risk is
highest.

\paragraph{Post-hoc calibration narrows but does not close the gap.}
Affine calibration improves the L1 and L2 baselines on the PLF cost
(L2: $0.428 \to 0.388$; L1: $0.407 \to 0.396$), confirming that a global
bias correction captures part of the asymmetric signal.
However, the end-to-end PLF model still achieves the lowest PLF cost
($0.386$), because it adapts the correction to individual input patterns
rather than applying a single global shift.
Figure~\ref{fig:error-heatmap} (Section~\ref{sec:forecast-error-dist})
illustrates exactly why: forecast error magnitude is far from uniform across
hours of the week, exhibiting a structured, hour- and day-dependent pattern.
A single affine correction applies the same rescaling to every prediction
regardless of when it falls; it cannot reduce errors selectively in the
high-error slots while leaving low-error slots unchanged.
Only a model that conditions its output on time of day and day of week ---
as our neural network does --- can adapt its correction to each slot
individually.
Notably, post-hoc calibration substantially \emph{worsens} the pinball model
on the PLF metric ($0.438 \to 1.309$): the affine recalibration overfits the
pinball model's bias to the training distribution in a way that does not
generalise to the PLF cost, highlighting the mismatch between the pinball and
PLF objectives.

\paragraph{Each model excels on its own training metric.}
As expected, L1 and L2 models achieve the lowest L1 and L2 costs
respectively, while the pinball model achieves the lowest pinball cost.
The PLF model, trained to minimise the piecewise linear asymmetric cost,
achieves the lowest PLF cost before and after calibration.
This confirms that the choice of training objective directly shapes the cost
profile of the resulting forecast, and that minimising a misaligned loss is
suboptimal when operational costs are asymmetric.

\paragraph{Error distributions.}
Figure~\ref{fig:error_histograms} shows the distribution of prediction errors
on the test set for each model.
The PLF model exhibits a pronounced rightward skew: it deliberately
over-predicts more often than it under-predicts, trading a slight increase in
average absolute error for a reduction in costly under-procurement events.
The L1 and L2 baselines produce near-zero-mean residuals with no systematic
bias, which is optimal under their respective training metrics but suboptimal
under the asymmetric PLF cost.

\paragraph{Per-metric breakdown.}
Every model achieves its lowest cost on the metric it was trained to minimise,
while the PLF model obtains the best PLF score at the expense of higher L1
and L2 costs.
This pattern is consistent with the quantitative results in
Table~\ref{tab:eval} and underscores the importance of aligning the
training objective with the operational cost structure.
